Investigating the convergence of Regge calculus\nThere are various ways in which the convergence properties of a numerical technique\ncan be explored.  The most rigorous approach involves the direct comparison of\nthe approximate equations and the full system, concentrating onthe leading order\ndiscrepancies. The underlying assumption is that the solutions of the approximate\nequations will converge at the same rate as the approximate equations themselves\nconverge to the original system.\nThe Regge and Einstein equations are too complex for such a direct approach to\nbe beneficial. Not only are the equations inherently complex non-linear systems, but\nbefore such an analysis can proceed one must choose a lattice uponwhich to express\nthe Regge equations, together with a coordinate system in which towrite out the\nEinstein equations. Once these decisions have been made, we are faced with a more\nproblematic choice: how are the two sets of equations to be compared? In general\nthere are more Regge equations (one per lattice edge) than Einstein equations (ten\nper spacetime event).\nThere is no clear prescription for averaging the lattice equations toobtain the\ncorrect number of Einstein equations in the continuum limit. Brewin [4]and M. Miller\n[5] have both developed their own schemes for averaging the Reggeequations.  A\ndirect assault on the convergence properties of Regge calculus is therefore likely to\nbe applicable only on a specific lattice, with a particular choice of averaging in the\ncontinuum limit. That is, the results would indicate that, in some averaged sense, the\n \nRegge equations converge to the Einstein equations. However, the result would not\nconstitute a proof valid for all possible lattice choices.\nGiven these limitations, it is natural to ask if similar results can be obtained\nby directly solving the Regge equations and comparing the lattice solutions to\ncorresponding Einstein spacetimes. Brewin and M. Miller have investigated the inverse\nproblem: given a known (analytic) solution of general relativity, andinterpolating that\nsolution onto a lattice, what can be said about the convergence of the Regge equations?\nThe approach taken by Brewin and M. Miller was to introduce some discretisation\nprocess in the smooth manifold on which the Einstein equations are defined. This\nprovides a way to map the smooth metric solutions to a lattice, giving aset of\nlattice edge lengthsL\nE\nderived from the continuum metric.  Both authors used\ngeodesic lengths to map continuum information onto the lattice spacetime.  These\nnew “continuum” lattice edge lengths do not in general satisfy the Regge equations,\nbut we can evaluate the residual\nr=|R(L\nE\n)|(1)\nwhich is an indication of how well the interpolated Einstein solution satisfies the Regge\nequations. It is this residual, using an appropriately chosen norm, which both Brewin\nand M. Miller considered [4, 5].\nBrewin observed that the residual of the simplicial equations remained roughly\nconstant as the lattice was refined on a fixed region of spacetime.  This result led\nBrewin to question the validity of Regge calculus, since any useful numerical scheme\nmust converge to the underlying solution of the partial differentialequations as the\nresolution is improved. M. Miller observed second order convergence of the residual,\nfor any smooth metric, whether or not they were solutions of Einstein’s equations.\nHad he used the same norm for the residual as used by Brewin we believe he too\nwould have observedO(1) convergence of the residuals.\nIt is important to note that the experiments of Brewin and M. Miller donot\ndirectly evaluate the convergence of the numerical solutions. Rather, they investigate\nthe convergence of the lattice equations to the Einstein equations. As the lattice is\nrefined, it is reasonable to expect that the interpolated Einstein solution will satisfy the\nRegge equations increasingly accurately. This, however, is not what Brewin observed\nnumerically.\n3. A possible explanation\nThe observations of Brewin are particularly puzzling in light of the many previous\napplications of Regge calculus.  Almost every numerical application ofthe method\nhas displayed convergence towards the corresponding continuumsolution, with\nmost studies indicating that numerical Regge calculus is a second order accurate\napproximation to general relativity.\nThe edge lengths measured in the Regge lattice (L) and the corresponding\ninterpolated Einstein edges (L\nE\n, obtained by assigning geodesic lengths calculated\nin the continuum) are related as\nL=L\nE\n+O(δ\np+1\n)(2)\nwith previous numerical studies suggesting thatp= 2 (see Gentle [3] for case studies\nand a general review). The key to Brewin’s proposal is allowing the functional form of\nthe error terms (the discrepancy between the Einstein and Reggesolutions) to depend\non the discretisation scaleδ. The discrepancy between the two solutions in this toy model is seen\nto be a wave-like disturbance with frequency proportional to 1/δ.\nIn the continuum limit it is reasonable to expect that the discrete Regge equations\n(or a weighted average over them) approach a system of differential equations.\nMoreover, consideration of the Einstein equations leads us to expect that the limiting\nform of the Regge equations is a set of second order non-linear equations. Furthermore,\nprevious numerical experiments suggest that Regge calculus is a second order method;\nwe expectp= 2. Conclusion\nWe have carried out a rigorous convergence study of a particular solution to the Regge\nequations, and shown that the solutions do indeed converge to thecorresponding\nEinstein solutions in the limit of very fine discretisations.\n \nFollowing a suggestion of Brewin we examined the behaviour of the error terms,\nand found convincing evidence for the existence of high frequency, low amplitude\noscillations superimposed upon the continuum solution. Brewin previously suggested\nthat waves of precisely this form in the simplicial solution could explain the lack of\nconvergence observed in the residual of the Regge equations when they are evaluated\non the interpolated continuum solution.\nTogether these results suggest that solutions of the Regge equations are generally\nsecond order accurate approximations to the corresponding Einstein spacetimes, with\nthe discrepancy between the two solutions consisting of high frequency, low amplitude\nwaves. Although these waves prevent the residual of the Regge equations converging\nto zero when evaluated on interpolated Einstein solutions [4], they donot affect the\noverall second order accuracy of the simplicial solutions.\nWe expect that all generic Regge simulations in vacuum will produce similar\nresults.  That is, all simulations will contain high frequency oscillations, linked to\nthe inverse of the discretisation scale, which reduce in magnitude asthe second\npower of that scale. These oscillations do not appear to induce instabilities in the\nnumerical evolution of simplicial lattices. The first is to\nthe issue of gravitational radiation. When electromagnetic radiation is present,\none can characterize it as the high–frequency part of the electromagnetic field\n[25], and assign to it an energy density and momentum. This leads to an\nenergy–momentum tensor that then serves as a source of curvature in the\nEinstein field equations. The spontaneous structure growth in the expanding universe due to gravitational attraction appears to be contrary to all the statements about entropy in standard\ntextbooks [27]. The most likely macroscopic\nstates will be those that correspond to the largest numbers of microscopic\nstates; that is to the largest volumes of phase space.
