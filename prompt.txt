Therefore, significant\nresearch efforts have been devoted to understanding privacy policies. Earlier approaches (Costante\net al., 2012) designed rule-based pattern matching\ntechniques to extract specific types of information.\nUnder theUsable Privacy Project(Sadeh et al.,\n2013), several works have been done (Bhatia and\nBreaux, 2015; Wilson et al., 2016a,b; Sathyendra\net al., 2016; Bhatia et al., 2016; Hosseini et al.,\n2016; Mysore Sathyendra et al., 2017; Zimmeck\net al., 2019; Bannihatti Kumar et al., 2020).  Notable works leveraging NLP techniques include text\nalignment (Liu et al., 2014; Ramanath et al., 2014),\ntext classification (Wilson et al., 2016a; Harkous\net al., 2018; Zimmeck et al., 2019), and question answering (QA) (Shvartzshanider et al., 2018; Harkous et al., 2018; Ravichander et al., 2019; Ahmad\net al., 2020). Bokaie Hosseini et al. (2020) is the\nmost closest to our work that used named entity\nrecognition (NER) modeling technique to extract\nthird party entities mentioned in policy documents.\nOur proposedPolicyIEcorpus is distinct from\nthe previous privacy policies benchmarks:  OPP115 (Wilson et al., 2016a) uses a hierarchical annotation scheme to annotate text segments with a\nset of data practices and it has been used for multilabel classification (Wilson et al., 2016a; Harkous\net al., 2018) and question answering (Harkous et al.,\n2018; Ahmad et al., 2020); PrivacyQA (Ravichander et al., 2019) frame the QA task as identifying\na list of relevant sentences from policy documents.\nRecently,  Bui et al. (2021) created a dataset by\ntagging documents from OPP-115 for privacy practices and uses NER models to extract them.   In\ncontrast, PolicyIEis developed by following semantic parsing benchmarks, and we model the task\nfollowing the NLP literature.\nIntent Classification and Slot FillingVoice assistants and chat-bots frame the task of natural language understanding via classifying intents and filling slots given user utterances. Several benchmarks\nhave been proposed in literature covering several\ndomains,  and  languages  (Hemphill  et  al.,  1990;\nCoucke et al., 2018; Gupta et al., 2018; Upadhyay\net al., 2018; Schuster et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020;\nLi et al., 2020). Our proposedPolicyIEcorpus is\na new addition to the literature within the security\nand privacy domain.PolicyIEenables us to build\nconversational solutions that users can interact with\nand learn about privacy policies.\n6    Conclusion\nThis work aims to stimulate research on automating information extraction from privacy policies\nand reconcile it with users’ understanding of their\nrights.  We presentPolicyIE, an intent classification and slot filling benchmark on privacy policies\nwith two alternative neural approaches as baselines.\nWe perform a thorough error analysis to shed light\non the limitations of the two baseline approaches.\nWe hope this contribution would call for research\nefforts in the specialized privacy domain from both\nprivacy and NLP communities.\nAcknowledgments\nThe authors acknowledge the law students Michael\nRasmussen and Martyna Glaz at Fordham University who worked as annotators to make the development of this corpus possible. Any opinions, findings, conclusions, or recommendations expressed herein are\nthose of the authors, and do not necessarily reflect\nthose of the US Government or NSF.\nBroader Impact\nPrivacy and data breaches have a significant impact on individuals. In general, security breaches\nexpose the users to different risks such as financial loss (due to losing employment or business\nopportunities), physical risks to safety, and identity theft. Identity theft is among the most severe\nand fastest-growing crimes.   However,  the risks\ndue to data breaches can be minimized if the users\nknow their rights and how they can exercise them\nto protect their privacy.   This requires the users\nto read the privacy policies of websites they visit\nor the mobile applications they use.  As reading\nprivacy policies is a tedious task, automating privacy policy analysis reduces the burden of users.\nAutomating information extraction from privacy\npolicies empowers users to be aware of their data\ncollected  and  analyzed  by  service  providers  for\ndifferent purposes. Service providers collect consumer data at a massive scale and often fail to protect them, resulting in data breaches that have led to\nincreased attention towards data privacy and related\nrisks. Reading privacy policies to understand users’\nrights can help take informed and timely decisions\non safeguarding data privacy to mitigate the risks.\nDeveloping an automated solution to facilitate policy document analysis requires labeled examples,\nand thePolicyIEcorpus adds a new dimension to\nthe available datasets in the security and privacy domain. WhilePolicyIEenables us to train models to\nextract fine-grained information from privacy policies, the corpus can be coupled with other existing\nbenchmarks to build a comprehensive system. For\nexample, PrivacyQA corpus (Ravichander et al.,\n2019) combined withPolicyIEcan facilitate building QA systems that can answer questions with\nfine-grained details. If the\nfunction is differentiable w.r.t.  its parameters, gradient descent is a relatively efficient optimization\nmethod, since the computation of first-order partial derivatives w.r.t. all the parameters is of the same\ncomputational complexity as just evaluating the function. These classifiers power theSegment ClassifierandQuery Analyzer\nmodules of Fig. 1.  We use word embeddings and neural\nnetworks thanks to their proven advantages in text classification [23] over traditional techniques.\n4.1    Privacy-Specific Word Embeddings\nTraditional text classifiers use the words and their frequencies as the building block for their features.  They,\nhowever,  have  limited  generalization  power,  especially\nwhen the training datasets are limited in size and scope.\nFor  example,  replacing  the  word  “erase”  by  the  word\n“delete” can significantly change the classification result\nif “delete” was not in the classifier’s training set.\nWord   embeddings   solve   this   issue   by   extracting\ngeneric  word  vectors  from  a  large  corpus,  in  an  unsupervised manner, and enabling their use in new classification problems (a technique termedTransfer Learning).\nThe features in the classifiers become the word vectors\ninstead of the words themselves.   Hence,  two text segments composed of semantically similar words would be\nrepresented by two groups of word vectors (i.e., features)\nthat are close in the vector space.  This allows the text\nclassifier to account for words outside the training set, as\nlong as they are part of the large corpus used to train the\nword vectors.\nWhile general-purpose pre-trained embeddings, such\nas  Word2vec  [24]  and  GloVe  [25]  do  exist,  domainspecific embeddings result in better classification accuracy  [26].   Thus,  we  trained  custom  word  embeddings\nfor the privacy-policy domain. To that end, we created a\ncorpus of 130K privacy policies collected from apps on\nthe Google Play Store. These policies typically describe\nthe overall data practices of the apps’ companies.\nWe crawled the metadata of more than 1.4 million Android  apps  available  via  the  PlayDrone  project  [27]  to\nfind the links to 199,186 privacy policies.  We crawled\nthe web pages for these policies, retrieving 130,326 policies which returned an HTTP status code of 200.  Then,\nwe  extracted  the  textual  content  from  their  HTML  using the policy crawler described in Sec. 3. We will refer\nto this corpus as thePolicies Corpus.  Using this corpus,\nwe trained a word-embeddings model usingfastText[28].\nWe henceforth call this model thePolicies Embeddings.\nA major advantage of usingfastTextis that it allows training vectors forsubwords(or charactern-grams of sizes 3\nto 6) in addition to words. Hence, even if we have words\noutside our corpus, we can assign them vectors by combining the vectors of their constituent subwords.  This is\nvery useful in accounting for spelling mistakes that occur\nin applications that involve free-form user queries.\n4.2    Classification Dataset\nOur  Policies  Embeddings  provides  a  solid  starting\npoint to build robust classifiers. Although  the  OPP-115  taxonomy\ncovers a wide variety of privacy practices [11], there are\ncertain types of applications that it does not fully capture.   One mitigation is to usePolisisas an initial step\nin order to filter the relevant data at a high level before\napplying additional, application-specific text processing.\nAnother mitigation is to leveragePolisis’ modularity by\namending it with new categories/attributes and training\nthese new classes on the relevant annotated dataset.\nMoreover, Polisis,  like  any  automated  approach,  exhibits  instances  of  misclassification  that  should  be  accounted for in any application building on it. One way to\nmitigate this problem is using confidence scores, similar\nto that of Eq. (3) to convey the (un)certainty of a reported\nresult, whether it is an answer, an icon, or another form of\nshort notice.  Last but not least, Polisisis not guaranteed\nto be robust in handling an adversarially constructed privacy policy. An adversary could include valid and meaningful statements in the privacy policy, carefully crafted\nto misleadPolisis’ automated classifiers.   For example,\nan adversary can replace words, in the policy, with synonyms that are far in our embeddings space.  While the\nmodified policy has the same meaning, Polisismight misclassify the modified segments.\nDeployment:We provide three prototype web applications for end-users. The first is an application that visualizes the different aspects in the privacy policy, powered\nby the annotations fromPolisis(available as a web application and a browser extension for Chrome and Firefox).   The  second  is  a  chatbot  implementation  ofPriBotfor  answering  questions  about  privacy  policies  in\na  conversational  interface.   The  third  is  an  application\nfor  extracting  the  privacy  labels  from  several  policies,\ngiven  their  links.    These  applications  are  available  at\nhttps://pribot.org.\nLegal AspectsWe also want to stress the fact thatPolisis\nis not intendedto replace the legally-binding privacy policy.  Rather, it offers a complementary interface for privacy stakeholders to easily inquire the contents of a privacy policy.  Following the trend of automation in legal\nadvice [56], insurance claim resolution [57], and privacy\npolicy presentation [58, 16], third parties, such as automated legal services firms or regulators, can deployPolisisas a solution for their users. As is the standard in such\nsituations, these parties should amendPolisiswith a disclaimer specifying that it is based on automatic analysis\nand does not represent the actual service provider [59].\nCompanies  and  service  providers  can  internally  deploy  an  application  similar  toPriBotas  an  assistance\ntool for their customer support agents to handle privacyrelated inquiries.  Putting the human in the loop allows\nfor  a  favorable  trade-off  between  the  utility  ofPolisis\nand  its  legal  implications.   For  a  wider  discussion  on\nthe issues surrounding automated legal analysis, we refer the interested reader to the works of McGinnis and\nPearce [60] and Pasquale [61].\nPrivacy-Specificity of the Approach:Finally, our approach is uniquely tailored to the privacy domain both\nfrom the data perspective and from the model-hierarchy\nperspective. However, we envision that applications with\nsimilar needs would benefit from extensions of our approach, both on the classification level and the QA level.\n10    Related Work\nPrivacy Policy Analysis:There have been numerous attempts to create easy-to-navigate and alternative presentations of privacy policies. Others have proposed standards\nto push service providers to encode privacy policies in\na machine-readable format,  such as P3P [13],  but they\nhave not been adopted by browser developers and service providers.Polisishas the potential to automate the\ngeneration of a lot of these notices, without relying on\nthe respective parties to do it themselves.\nRecently, several researchers have explored the potential  of  automated  analysis  of  privacy  policies.   For  example, Liuet al.[58] have used deep learning to model\nthe  vagueness  of  words  in  privacy  policies. These studies, among others [64, 65], have been largely enabled by the release of\nthe  OPP-115  dataset  by  Wilsonet  al.[11],  containing\n115  privacy  policies  extensively  annotated  by  law  students.   Our  work  is  the  first  to  provide  a  generic  system  for  the  automated  analysis  of  privacy  policies.   In\nterms of the comprehensiveness and the accuracy of the\napproach, Polisismakes a major improvement over the\nstate of the art.  It allows transitioning from labeling of\npolicies  with  a  few  practices  (e.g.,  the  works  by  Zimmeck and Bellovin [16] and Sathyendraet al.[17]) to a\nmuch more fine-grained annotation (up to 10 high-level\nand 122 fine-grained classes), thus enabling a richer set\nof applications.\nEvaluating the Compliance Industry:Regulators and\nresearchers are continuously scrutinizing the practices of\nthe privacy compliance industry [21, 38, 39]. It can provide the\ninitial evidence that could be processed by skilled experts\nafterward, thus reducing the analysis time and the cost.\nAutomated Question Answering:Our QA system, PriBot, is focused onnon-factoidquestions, which are usually complex and open-ended.  Over the past few years,\ndeep learning has yielded superior results to traditional\nretrieval  techniques  in  this  domain  [51,  52,  66].   Our\nmain contribution is that we build a QA system,  without a dataset that includes questions and answers, while\nachieving results on par with the state of the art on other\ndomains.  We envision that our approach could be transplanted to other problems that face similar issues.\n11    Conclusion\nWe proposedPolisis, the first generic framework that\nenables detailed automatic analysis of privacy policies.\nIt can assist users, researchers, and regulators in processing and understanding the content of privacy policies at\nscale.   To buildPolisis,  we developed a new  hierarchy\nof neural networks that extracts both high-level privacy\npractices  as  well  as  fine-grained  information  from  privacy policies.  Using this extracted information, Polisis\nenables several applications.   In this paper,  we demonstrated two applications: structured and free-form querying.
